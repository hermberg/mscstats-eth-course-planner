Programme structure & credit rules

Ninety credits, seven buckets, and one condition that quietly governs the rest — core, electives and seminar must total 52 ECTS between them, which is 21 more than their individual minimums require.

12 ECTSCore courses — and at least one course in each of the three core areas: Mathematical Statistics, Statistical Modelling, Applied Statistics. Six core courses exist in total, all in this table. Five run in Autumn; only Computational Statistics runs in Spring.
15 ECTSSubject-specific electives — deepen expertise within statistics or closely related quantitative fields. Machine learning, high-dimensional statistics, risk management, optimization, advanced probability.
4 ECTSSeminar or semester project — read papers and present, or work an applied/theoretical project. StatsLab (Spring) places you on a real consulting case for an external partner; a semester paper can also satisfy this.
52 ECTSThe 52 rule — core + subject-specific electives + seminar must sum to at least 52 ECTS combined. This is the binding constraint. The three individual minimums only add to 31, so roughly 21 further ECTS must come from these three buckets.
2 ECTSGESS — Science in Perspective — ethics, philosophy of science, law, history, political and social sciences. Not listed in these two catalogues; the section links out to the separate GESS catalogue.
30 ECTSMaster's thesis — substantial independent research project, theoretical or applied, normally in the final stage.
6 ECTSFree electives — the remainder, from almost any department. Also not enumerated in these catalogues. The structured requirements above reach 84 ECTS.
90 ECTSTotal required to graduate
The one-per-area rule binds harder than the 12 ECTS. The cheapest legal core combination is Time Series Analysis (4) + Applied Analysis of Variance (5) + Likelihood and Regression I (7) = 16 ECTS, which already clears 12. So treat the core requirement as "one from each area", not as a credit target — and note that Time Series Analysis is biennial and Likelihood and Regression I is hosted at UZH.
Time-critical for Autumn 2026. Likelihood and Regression I is one of only two courses in the Mathematical Statistics core area, and it is hosted at UZH — taking it requires a mobility application whose deadline is the Friday before the lecture period begins. The UZH lecture period runs 14 September – 19 December 2026, putting that deadline on or around Friday 11 September 2026. If that passes, the only remaining Mathematical Statistics option is Fundamentals of Mathematical Statistics (9 ECTS, proof-based, the lowest average and pass rate of the six core courses). Verify the exact date with UZH.

Registration, withdrawal and exam rules

  • You may register for up to 12 exams per semester, normally in the first weeks of the semester.
  • You can withdraw from an exam up to roughly 10 days before the exam date, and a timely withdrawal leaves no trace on your transcript. Registering broadly and cutting later is standard practice.
  • ETH typically offers one exam session per course per year. A failure counts as one attempt; you may retake once. After two failed attempts the course cannot be taken again.
  • Exam dates are announced late — usually around four weeks before the end of the semester, and not all courses sit in a stable slot year to year.
  • Grades run 1 to 6 in 0.25 increments; 4.0 passes. Passing is generally not the hard part — scoring high is.
  • Cherry-picking is permitted. Once the ECTS requirements are met you may keep taking exams in the same areas and select the best results for the diploma transcript. Every attempt, passed or failed, still appears on the full academic record.
  • UZH exams count for free electives, and a few are recognised in the core areas — Likelihood and Regression I among them. This requires a mobility application, and the deadline is the Friday before the lecture period begins.
  • There is no overall exam limit and no 90-credit ceiling. You may stay up to three years; around two years with an integrated internship is typical, and 1.5 years is possible but background-dependent.

Choosing courses

  • Pick one course per core area early, add one or two electives according to workload tolerance, then adjust within the first weeks.
  • Do not overload the first semester — particularly coming from an economics, engineering or other applied background, where proof-based courses are the steeper climb. Starting conservatively and scaling up beats overcommitting.
  • Preparation reading suggested by the student reps: Schaum's Outline of Advanced Calculus (3rd ed.); Schaum's Outline of Linear Algebra (6th ed.); Blitzstein & Hwang, Introduction to Probability (2nd ed.); Tao, An Introduction to Measure Theory — measure-theoretic vocabulary appears in several courses even where deep mastery is not required.
